# Ondansetron

Welcome to Mechanism Diligence.
Let’s talk about ondansetron, which is the generic name.
Zofran is a brand name, and it may be the name you already know.

## What is this drug?

Ondansetron is an antiemetic: it prevents nausea and vomiting in specified settings. We’ll follow swallowed oral tablets for adults receiving moderately emetogenic chemotherapy. Emetogenic means capable of provoking vomiting.

## What biological problem does it address?

Chemotherapy can release serotonin from cells in the intestinal lining. Serotonin activates sensory nerve endings, including vagal afferents that carry signals toward the brainstem. Those signals help recruit the network coordinating vomiting. Nausea and vomiting are related but distinct outcomes.

## How does it reach its target?

The tablet dissolves. After intestinal absorption and first-pass metabolism, the remaining drug reaches the systemic circulation. Targets include peripheral vagal endings and central sites involved in emetic signaling. Ondansetron does not coat the stomach or block chemotherapy delivery.

## What happens at the target?

The target is the five-HT-three serotonin receptor. Unlike other serotonin receptor families, it is an ion channel. Serotonin binding outside the cell promotes opening, allowing positive ions to cross the membrane and excite the neuron.

Ondansetron competes at the serotonin-binding pocket and prevents effective channel activation. It is not a plug in the channel’s central pore. It is also not a dopamine receptor blocker.

## How does that action change the system?

Reduced receptor activation lowers one excitatory input into vomiting circuits. Peripheral blockade reduces signaling from the gut; central blockade may contribute too.

Other pathways remain active. Substance P, for example, acts through a different receptor and contributes especially to delayed symptoms. This helps explain the use of combination prevention regimens.

## What is established, and what is still uncertain?

Five-HT-three antagonism is established. The label says the full antiemetic mechanism is not completely characterized. The relative contribution of peripheral and central sites remains uncertain.

## How does the action connect to benefit and harm?

Blocking serotonin-driven signaling can prevent vomiting. Effects on gut signaling can also contribute to constipation. Symptom control does not establish that bowel movement has recovered.

QT prolongation and dangerous arrhythmias are separate cardiac risks. QT is an electrical interval on the ECG. The label also warns about myocardial ischemia, reduced blood flow to heart muscle; new chest pain needs urgent assessment.

## How long does the action last?

Protection depends on the emetic stimulus, dosing schedule, and other medicines. Plasma half-life is not a universal protection timer. The liver metabolizes most of the drug; severe hepatic impairment reduces clearance.

## What adult dosing does the label describe?

For adult moderately emetogenic chemotherapy, the oral label describes eight milligrams thirty minutes beforehand, another eight milligrams eight hours after the first dose, then eight milligrams every twelve hours for one to two days after chemotherapy ends. With severe hepatic impairment, do not exceed eight milligrams total daily. These are label regimens, not personal dosing advice.

## What must not be overlooked?

Ondansetron is contraindicated with apomorphine and with hypersensitivity to the drug or its ingredients. Separately, avoid it in congenital long-QT syndrome. Warnings include serotonin syndrome, especially with serotonergic drugs, and masking progressive ileus: worsening failure of intestinal propulsion.

## What is the take-home mechanism?

Ondansetron blocks serotonin’s activation of an excitatory ion channel. It reduces one route into the vomiting network without treating every cause of nausea or vomiting.
