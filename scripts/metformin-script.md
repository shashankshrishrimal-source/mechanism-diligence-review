## 1. What is this drug?

Welcome to Mechanism Diligence.
You might be here to understand a drug you were prescribed, or to learn how it works.
This one is metformin.
Glucophage is the brand name, and it may be the name you already know.
This film follows immediate-release oral tablets, for adults with type 2 diabetes, together with diet and exercise.

Scene: black. Pure-white MD mark, top left, not stretched, D open. “Mechanism Diligence” under the mark, smaller. Centered header “Mechanism Diligence” only on this opening. Large name, brand in brackets. Small chemical structure captioned “chemical structure.” Small teal 3D model captioned “3D representative structure.” One route: oral immediate-release tablets.

## 2. What is high blood sugar here?

Type 2 diabetes means the body does not use insulin well.
The liver can also release too much glucose.
Glucose is the sugar in the blood.
Blood sugar stays high.
You cannot feel the number.
Only a blood test confirms it.
Over years, high sugar can damage eyes, kidneys, nerves, and the heart.

Scene: glucose particles in blood. No drug. Define glucose on the first picture. Source: label 1, 12.1.

## 3. What does the drug do?

Metformin is a biguanide.
It lowers fasting and after-meal blood sugar.
It does this without making the pancreas release more insulin.
The label names three effects.
It decreases glucose production in the liver.
It decreases intestinal absorption of glucose.
It improves insulin sensitivity, so muscle and fat take up more glucose.

Scene: three short shots, one effect each. Liver, gut, muscle. Metformin is teal. Glucose is icy. Source: label 12.1.

## 4. Where does the work happen?

Most of the labeled action is in the liver.
Liver cells make new glucose between meals.
Metformin decreases that output.
The gut and the muscle are part of the same labeled effect.
The label does not name one enzyme.
We are changing the scale of the picture, not watching the medicine jump.
The shapes are not the true shapes and are not to scale.

Scene: liver, then a hepatocyte, glucose leaving more slowly. No invented enzyme. Source: label 12.1.

## 5. What changes on the test?

Fasting and after-meal sugar can fall.
The effect builds over days to weeks.
Low blood sugar is uncommon when metformin is used alone.
It works only while it is taken.
It does not cure the diabetes.
Reducing liver glucose output and improving insulin sensitivity lowers blood sugar. That is the same action that can raise the risk of lactic acidosis when the kidneys are impaired.
The drug itself leaves mainly through the kidneys.
That is why severe kidney impairment is a limit.

Scene: fewer glucose particles in blood. No clock drawn as a measurement. Source: label 12.1, 5.

## 6. What dose does the label list?

For adults, the immediate-release label starts at 500 milligrams twice a day, or 850 milligrams once a day, with meals.
The dose can rise gradually.
The maximum is 2,550 milligrams a day, in divided doses.
These are label limits, not a dose chosen for you.
Extended-release tablets have different instructions.

Scene: three-line card. 500 or 850 start. 2,550 maximum, divided. Label limits. Source: label 2.

## 7. What are the limits?

Do not use it if the kidneys are severely impaired.
Do not use it in metabolic acidosis, including diabetic ketoacidosis.
Do not use it after a serious allergy to metformin.
There is a boxed warning for lactic acidosis.
It is rare, and it can be life-threatening.
Kidney function is checked.
Lactic acidosis is the same-action risk the label boxes.
This film is for education, not personal medical advice.
Independent clinical review is pending.
For the sources, and to leave feedback, go to mechanismdiligence.com.

Scene: limits card. Boxed lactic acidosis named, not illustrated as a face. Confirm the eGFR cut against the exact label before filming. If the label says eGFR below 30, say that. If it does not, say “severely impaired” only. Source: label 4, 5.1.
