Welcome to Mechanism Diligence.
You might be here to understand a drug you were prescribed, or to learn how it works.
This one is rosuvastatin.
Crestor is the brand name, and it may be the name you already know.
This film follows oral rosuvastatin calcium tablets.

## 2. What is high LDL?

Hyperlipidemia means too much fat-carrying material in the blood.
The part this film follows is LDL cholesterol.
LDL is a particle that carries cholesterol.
HDL is a smaller particle.
You cannot feel the number.
Only a blood test confirms it.
The label also covers reducing major heart and stroke events in adults at increased risk.
That is a different plan.

Scene: LDL particles only. No drug. If HDL is shown, it is smaller and a different color. Source: label 1.

## 3. What can years of high LDL do?

If high LDL continues for years, cholesterol can build up in artery walls.
That can narrow blood flow.
There are no early signs without a blood test.

Scene: an artery wall with a deposit. No face. No drug.

## 4. What does the drug block?

Rosuvastatin is a statin.
It blocks HMG-CoA reductase.
That enzyme turns a building block into mevalonate.
Mevalonate is a step toward cholesterol.
Rosuvastatin is shaped to sit in that enzyme.
Nothing is aiming.
The shape fits.
The block lets go.
The shapes are not the true shapes and are not to scale.

Scene: teal enzyme. Gold drug seated. Small gold specks leaving, labeled “Mevalonate.” Left clump “Earlier enzymes.” Right clump “Later enzymes.” Do not letter LDL or transporters on this picture. Source: label 12.1.

## 5. Where does the work happen?

Most of the action is in the liver.
Liver cells make most of the body’s cholesterol.
The view is getting closer.
We are changing the scale of the picture, not watching the medicine jump.

Scene: liver, then hepatocytes. Source: label 12.1.

## 6. How does it enter the liver cell?

Rosuvastatin is a substrate of OATP1B1.
It is also a substrate of BCRP.
Those are the named transporters in the label.
Medicines that block them can raise rosuvastatin levels.

Scene: hepatocyte membrane. Violet OATP1B1. Copper BCRP. Gold drug crossing. Source: label 12.3.

## 7. What happens after the block?

Less mevalonate means less new cholesterol is made.
The liver puts more LDL receptors on the cell surface.
Those receptors take more LDL back from the blood.
The label also says liver VLDL synthesis falls, so fewer particles are sent out.
The maximum LDL drop is usually reached by 4 weeks.
It is maintained after that.
It works only while the drug is taken.
The 4-week LDL clock is not a blood-level clock.
The label does not give a half-life for the cholesterol change.
Most of the drug leaves in feces.
A smaller fraction leaves in urine.

Scene: receptors on the membrane. Icy LDL particles entering the cell. No animal-model claim. The label states the receptor step as pharmacodynamics. Source: label 12.2.

## 8. What dose does the label list?

For adults, the labeled range is 5 to 40 milligrams once a day.
The dose depends on the indication, the LDL number, and the person’s risk.
The maximum on this label is 40 milligrams once a day.
These are label limits, not a dose chosen for you.
Severe kidney impairment uses a different start and a lower ceiling.
That plan is not this film.

Scene: three-line card. 5 to 40 mg. Once a day. Label limits. Source: label 2.2.

## 9. What are the limits?

Do not use it in acute liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis.
Do not use it after a serious allergy to rosuvastatin or to an ingredient in the tablet.
Muscle injury can be serious.
Liver-enzyme increases have occurred.
It may cause fetal harm.
Muscle injury is the same-action harm the label warns for.
This film is for education, not personal medical advice.
Independent clinical review is pending.
For the sources, and to leave feedback, go to mechanismdiligence.com.

Scene: limits card. Source: label 4, 5.1, 5.3, 8.1.
